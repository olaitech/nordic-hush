# THE LONG WINTER

## A Viking-Inspired Sleep Story in Four Chapters

# Chapter One — Before the Snow Came

Far in the north, where steep mountains rose from the sea and narrow fjords reached deep into the land, there stood a small settlement beside the water.

A handful of wooden houses rested between the shoreline and the forest.

Beyond them were fields that had already been harvested, patches of pale grass, and dark stands of birch and pine.

Above the roofs, thin trails of smoke drifted into the cool autumn air.

Boats rested on the shore.

Fishing nets hung from wooden poles.

Bundles of rope had been coiled beneath small shelters.

And high on the mountains, the first snow of the coming winter had already settled.

It was late autumn.

Each morning, the sun climbed a little less high above the horizon.

Each evening, the shadows reached across the settlement a little sooner.

The people who lived beside the fjord knew what those changes meant.

Winter was approaching.

There would be deep snow.

There would be frozen streams and cold winds.

There would be long evenings beside the hearth and mornings that began in darkness.

The people did not speak of winter as something to conquer.

It was simply part of the year.

Something that came in its own time.

Something that required preparation, patience, and care.

So while the last yellow leaves still clung to the birch trees, the settlement was busy.

Near the shore lived a farmer named Eirik.

He was no longer young.

His hair, once pale gold, had become mostly silver.

His beard was thick, and the wind from the fjord had left fine lines around his eyes.

Eirik had watched many winters arrive.

Every autumn, when the first frost appeared on the grass, he said the same thing.

"Winter begins before the snow."

His daughter Astrid had heard those words many times.

Even so, she smiled whenever he said them.

That morning, Astrid was stacking split birch beside the longhouse.

She placed each piece carefully against the next.

The wood was dry and pale beneath its rough bark.

When she lifted it, she could smell the faint sweetness of birch.

Her younger brother, Leif, carried smaller pieces in a woven basket.

He crossed the frost-covered ground slowly, his boots making a soft, steady crunch beneath him.

The woodpile was already almost as tall as Astrid.

Still, Eirik wanted more.

He stood nearby, splitting another log with practiced movements.

"A fire uses plenty of wood when the north wind comes," he said.

Astrid set another piece onto the stack.

"And you say the north wind always comes."

Eirik gave a small nod.

"Sooner or later."

Leif lowered his basket and looked up at the gray sky.

"Maybe it will forget this year."

Eirik smiled.

"I would not depend on that."

Nearby, their mother, Sigrid, was preparing the last of the autumn food for storage.

Fish caught during the warmer months had been dried on wooden racks.

Some had become firm and light, ready to be kept through the cold season.

Other fish had been salted and stored away.

There were sacks of barley.

Baskets of turnips.

Bundles of dried herbs.

Hazelnuts gathered from the woods.

Dried berries.

Small rounds of cheese.

And jars of butter kept in the coolest part of the house.

Everything had its place.

Summer had provided what it could.

Now the family carefully stored what remained.

Inside the longhouse, bunches of herbs hung from the rafters.

Dry leaves shifted gently whenever warm air moved through the room.

Bundles of wool waited beside the loom.

Sheepskins covered the benches.

Heavy blankets had been folded and stacked where they could be reached easily.

The building itself had been checked carefully.

Small gaps between timbers had been filled.

The shutters fitted closely.

The roof had been repaired after the autumn rains.

Around the rest of the settlement, other families were making similar preparations.

Someone repaired the wall of an animal shelter.

Someone dragged a boat higher onto the shore.

A man inspected the bindings on a wooden sled.

Two women carried baskets from a storage building.

Children collected fallen branches from the edge of the forest.

Older people sat near doorways, mending woolen clothing and checking leather straps.

The work continued without hurry.

There was still time.

But no one wished to waste it.

Even the dogs wandered between the houses as if they understood that the season was changing.

They sniffed around the woodpiles, watched people carrying baskets, and occasionally settled in a patch of weak sunlight.

Above the settlement, ravens crossed the pale sky.

Their dark wings moved slowly against the clouds.

By midday, those clouds had grown heavier.

Astrid noticed them while carrying another armful of wood.

They were low and broad, the color of cold iron.

The mountain peaks had disappeared behind them.

Eirik followed her gaze.

"It may snow tonight," he said.

Leif looked upward with interest.

"How much?"

Eirik rested the handle of his axe against his shoulder.

"The sky has not told me."

Leif considered this.

"Could you ask?"

Astrid laughed softly.

Eirik looked very serious.

"I have tried. The sky is not known for answering."

The afternoon passed beneath the darkening clouds.

Tools were gathered and brought under shelter.

The animals were moved closer to the buildings.

Fishing lines were rolled and stored.

A wooden cart was covered.

Doors were checked once more.

The last bundles of firewood were carried nearer to the longhouse.

When the light began to fade, families gradually disappeared indoors.

Inside Eirik's longhouse, the central hearth gave off a steady warmth.

Orange firelight moved across the timber walls.

Smoke rose toward the opening above.

The room smelled of woodsmoke, wool, and the evening meal.

A pot hung above the fire.

Inside it, barley simmered with onion, turnip, herbs, and small pieces of salted meat.

Tiny bubbles rose and broke softly on the surface.

Sigrid stirred the stew with a wooden spoon.

Astrid sat near the hearth, repairing a loose seam in one of Leif's mittens.

Leif himself was carving a small wooden horse.

He worked carefully, turning the piece over in his hands before making each cut.

Eirik sat opposite him and sharpened a knife with a smooth stone.

The quiet movement of stone against metal blended with the crackling fire.

Outside, the wind began to move around the longhouse.

At first it was only a gentle breath beneath the eaves.

Then it grew into a long, low murmur.

Leif stopped carving for a moment.

"Is that the snow?"

"Only the wind," Astrid said.

A little later, something soft touched the wooden shutter.

Then something else.

Eirik looked toward the wall.

He set down the knife and stood.

At the doorway, he lifted the wooden bar and opened the door just enough to look outside.

Cold air slipped into the room.

Astrid felt it against her face.

It carried the clean, sharp smell of winter.

Eirik held out one hand.

A snowflake landed on his skin.

Another settled on his sleeve.

Then several more turned slowly through the darkness beyond the doorway.

He closed the door again and replaced the bar.

"The first snow," he said.

For a little while, no one spoke.

They simply listened.

The fire settled gently in the hearth.

The stew continued to simmer.

A dog stretched beside the wall and lowered its head onto its paws.

Outside, soft snow began to brush against the roof.

Winter had arrived quietly.

After supper, the family stayed near the fire.

Their bowls were empty, and the iron pot had been moved away from the hottest part of the hearth.

Leif sat beneath a wool blanket.

Astrid worked on another piece of mending.

Sigrid sorted small bundles of dried herbs.

Eirik began telling stories.

They were not stories of kings or distant battles.

They were stories of winters he remembered.

He told them about a year when the snow had climbed almost to the roof of his father's house.

He remembered a season when a sheltered part of the fjord had frozen so firmly that neighboring families had crossed it on foot.

He told Leif about a fox that once stole a fish from beside the smokehouse and returned several nights later to see whether another had been left for it.

"Did you leave one?" Leif asked.

"No."

"Did it come back?"

"Yes."

"Then perhaps it thought you had forgotten."

"Perhaps."

Astrid smiled and pulled the blanket more tightly around her shoulders.

Outside, the snow continued falling.

It covered the path between the houses.

It settled over the grass.

It softened the shape of the woodpiles.

It gathered along fences and covered the roofs of the boats pulled onto the shore.

The familiar ground disappeared slowly beneath white.

By the middle of the night, the whole settlement had changed.

The dark earth was gone.

The stones beside the houses had vanished.

The fields had become broad, pale spaces beneath the clouds.

Inside the longhouse, the fire grew smaller.

One by one, the family prepared for sleep.

Sheepskins lay beneath their blankets.

Extra wool coverings were placed within reach.

The dogs curled together near the warm stones around the hearth.

Sigrid moved the last glowing pieces of wood closer together.

A small flame rose briefly and settled again.

The room became quiet.

Astrid lay beneath her blankets and listened.

She could hear the faint movement of wind beyond the wall.

She could hear Leif breathing nearby.

She could hear the soft settling of burning wood.

Far away, somewhere beyond the snowy trees, a wolf called once through the darkness.

The sound was distant.

Then the forest became quiet again.

Astrid closed her eyes.

The longhouse was warm.

The snow continued to fall beyond the roof.

And the first night of winter passed gently over the northern fjord.

# Chapter Two — The Deep Winter

The weeks passed.

Snow gathered across the settlement until the paths between houses became narrow, well-trodden passages.

The roofs carried thick white coverings.

Small streams disappeared beneath ice.

The mountains became pale under the winter sky.

Along the sheltered edges of the fjord, thin ice appeared and slowly widened.

The days shortened further.

For a while, the sun barely lifted above the distant ridges.

Its light touched the mountains in soft gold and faded again.

Most of the day rested in blue-gray twilight.

The nights belonged to stars.

Still, the settlement continued with its quiet routines.

Each morning, Eirik woke before the others.

He moved through the dim longhouse without speaking.

At the hearth, he knelt beside the ashes and carefully stirred them.

Beneath the gray surface, a few embers often remained.

He gathered them close.

Then he added a little dry material and several thin pieces of wood.

Eirik leaned forward and breathed gently across the coals.

Their glow brightened.

A small flame appeared.

Soon another followed.

Before long, the familiar warmth of morning fire began to spread through the room.

Sigrid usually woke next.

Then Astrid.

Then Leif, who often remained beneath his blankets until he smelled breakfast.

No one hurried.

Winter mornings had a slower rhythm.

There was no reason to rush into darkness.

Breakfast was simple.

Warm barley porridge.

Perhaps a little dried fruit.

Sometimes a spoonful of honey.

Steam rose from the wooden bowls as they ate.

Hands wrapped around the warm sides.

Conversation remained quiet.

When the sky outside became lighter, the day's work began.

Snow had to be cleared where it gathered too heavily on roofs.

Firewood had to be carried indoors.

Water had to be collected.

Animals needed food.

Stored provisions needed checking.

Clothing required mending.

Small jobs filled the hours.

The sheep remained in a sheltered enclosure during the coldest weather.

Their wool had grown thick.

Their breath formed pale clouds when the door opened.

Hay collected during summer was carried to them each day.

The goats received hay as well, along with bundles of leafy branches that had been cut and dried months earlier.

Astrid often helped feed them.

The goats learned the sound of her arrival.

Whenever she entered the shelter with an armful of feed, they turned toward her.

One older goat named Bruna always tried to reach her first.

"You behave as though no one has fed you since midsummer," Astrid murmured.

Bruna simply continued chewing.

Outside the shelter, snow drifted quietly from the roof.

Some winter days were calm.

On those days, the world seemed very still.

Snow softened footsteps and distant voices.

The trees stood beneath white branches.

Smoke rose almost straight upward from the houses.

The fjord lay dark where the water remained open, and pale where thin ice had formed along the shore.

Astrid sometimes walked down to the water when her work was finished.

She would stand there for a few minutes and look across the fjord.

The opposite mountains appeared blue in the winter light.

Far away, smoke rose from another farm.

A raven might cross the sky, beating its wings steadily before disappearing beyond the forest.

On other days, the wind came down from the mountains.

Loose snow moved across the open ground.

Old footprints disappeared.

Drifts formed beside walls.

The shutters trembled gently beneath stronger gusts.

When the weather was unpleasant, everyone stayed close to home.

Then the longhouse became a small world of its own.

There was always work to occupy quiet hands.

Wool was spun.

Clothing was repaired.

Wooden bowls and spoons were carved.

Hooks and tools were checked.

Ropes were examined and repaired.

Children practiced carving simple runes into small scraps of wood.

Nothing was hurried.

Long winter evenings left plenty of time.

Near his sleeping place, Eirik kept a wooden chest.

Inside it were small objects gathered over many years.

There was a bronze buckle.

A smooth stone from a beach far from their fjord.

A broken piece of green glass.

A small silver coin.

A comb made from antler.

A few carved pieces whose stories Leif could never quite remember.

Leif loved the chest.

Every few evenings, he asked Eirik to open it.

Eirik always pretended to think carefully about the request.

"Perhaps," he would say.

Leif waited.

Eirik rubbed his beard.

"Perhaps one thing."

Leif usually chose the green glass.

He held it near the fire so the light passed through.

The glow turned his fingers green.

"Where did it come from?" he asked one evening.

"A trader brought it from far to the south," said Eirik.

"How far?"

Eirik looked into the fire.

"Far enough that I would rather not walk there."

Leif thought about this.

"Could I go?"

"Perhaps."

"When?"

"When winter is over."

Leif frowned slightly.

"Winter takes too long."

Eirik smiled.

"That is because you are waiting for summer."

The fire burned quietly between them.

Sigrid continued spinning wool.

Astrid listened while working a knot loose from a piece of cord.

Outside, snow rested against the walls.

Inside, the evening remained warm.

As the season deepened, the cold became stronger.

One morning, Eirik stepped outside and knew at once that the night had been especially cold.

The air was completely still.

His breath gathered as frost along his beard.

The snow beneath his boots felt dry and firm.

The trees beyond the settlement did not move.

A pale mist floated low above the open water farther out in the fjord.

On such days, everyone paid closer attention to small things.

Doors were kept closed as much as possible.

Young people carried extra wood to older neighbors.

The animals were checked more often.

Water was brought indoors before containers could freeze.

Fire was tended carefully.

No household was entirely alone.

That evening, Eirik's family sat close to the hearth.

Even the dogs remained near the warm stones.

Sigrid cooked flat pieces of barley bread on a heated surface beside the fire.

The smell of warm bread filled the room.

Astrid added dried herbs to a pot of broth.

Leif sat with his feet tucked beneath his blanket.

"How cold is it?" he asked.

Eirik looked toward the shutter.

"Cold enough that I am pleased to be sitting here."

Leif considered that answer.

"Will the house stay warm?"

"It should."

"The fire too?"

"The fire most of all."

Eirik pointed gently around the room.

"The walls help. The wool helps. The food helps. And we help one another."

Leif nodded.

That seemed enough for him.

After eating, they made the longhouse ready for the night.

The shutters were checked.

An extra wool covering was placed near the doorway.

Everyone slept closer to the central hearth.

Outside, the cold settled over the fields and forest.

Inside the far corners of the longhouse, a delicate line of frost appeared on one beam.

Near the fire, however, the stones remained warm.

Soft orange light moved across the walls.

The family slept beneath wool and sheepskin.

The dogs breathed quietly.

The embers glowed through the night.

Above the roof, the sky had cleared.

Stars covered the darkness from one mountain ridge to the other.

Later that night, something pale appeared among them.

Astrid woke and noticed a faint green light through a narrow space near the shutter.

For a few moments, she remained beneath her blanket.

Then curiosity drew her up.

She wrapped herself in a thick wool cloak and crossed the room quietly.

At the doorway, she lifted the bar, stepped outside, and closed the door behind her.

The cold touched her face at once.

The settlement was silent.

Snow covered the roofs.

No one else seemed awake.

Astrid looked upward.

Green light stretched across the sky.

At first it was faint.

Then it grew brighter.

Long curtains of color moved above the mountains.

They shifted slowly, almost without sound, folding and unfolding across the stars.

Some parts were pale green.

Others seemed silver.

A few edges carried the faintest blue.

Astrid stood very still.

Her breath rose in small white clouds.

Behind her, the longhouse held the warmth of the sleeping family.

Before her, the entire northern sky seemed to move.

She watched for a while.

There was no need to understand it.

No need to speak.

The moment was enough.

At last, the cold encouraged her back inside.

Astrid closed the door and replaced the wooden bar.

She crossed to the hearth.

One small log was enough to brighten the embers.

A soft flame appeared along its edge.

She returned to her sleeping place.

The roof gave a quiet creak as the cold tightened the timbers.

Leif shifted beneath his blanket but did not wake.

Sigrid and Eirik slept nearby.

Astrid closed her eyes.

Outside, the green light continued its slow passage over the mountains.

Inside, the fire glowed.

And the deep winter night continued peacefully around the little settlement.

# Chapter Three — The Great Storm

The storm gave several quiet signs before it arrived.

Eirik noticed the wind changing first.

It began to come from the northwest.

Clouds gathered beyond the mouth of the fjord.

Sea birds that usually remained near the coast appeared farther inland.

The air had a heavy stillness beneath the moving clouds.

Eirik stood near the shore and watched the horizon.

Astrid came to stand beside him.

She tucked her hands into her sleeves.

"You think there is weather coming."

Eirik nodded.

"A strong winter storm, I expect."

Astrid looked toward the houses.

There was no fear in the settlement.

There was simply more work to do.

Before afternoon had passed, families were preparing.

Additional firewood was carried indoors.

Water containers were filled.

The animals were given plenty of feed and settled behind secure doors.

Loose tools were moved under shelter.

Wooden shutters were checked.

Roof edges were examined.

Doors were made ready for several days of wind and snow.

Sigrid inspected the food stored inside the longhouse.

Astrid carried in another basket of split wood.

Leif helped by bringing small pieces that could be used to start the fire.

By evening, everything that could reasonably be done had been done.

The family ate before the weather arrived.

They had warm broth, bread, and a little cheese.

The dogs rested beside the hearth.

Beyond the shutter, the sky had become dark.

The first stronger wind came after sunset.

It moved along the walls in a low, steady gust.

A little later, snow began to brush against the roof and shutters.

Leif paused while pulling off his boots.

"Has it started?"

Eirik listened for a moment.

"I think so."

"Will it be a large storm?"

"Large enough that we can spend tomorrow indoors."

Leif looked toward the hearth.

He seemed pleased by this.

"All day?"

"Perhaps."

Astrid smiled.

"Then you may finally finish that wooden horse."

Leif had been carving it for weeks.

Every few days he decided that one part needed changing.

The storm strengthened through the night.

Wind flowed down from the mountains and moved around the longhouse.

Snow gathered along the sheltered side of the building.

The roof timbers creaked softly beneath the changing pressure.

Inside, the hearth remained bright.

Leif woke once during the night.

Astrid was also awake.

She could hear the wind beyond the walls.

"Is everything all right?" he whispered.

"Everything is fine."

Eirik's voice came quietly from near the fire.

"The house has heard winter winds before."

He added another piece of wood to the hearth.

Small sparks rose and disappeared into the dimness above.

Leif settled back beneath his blankets.

The sound outside became another part of the night.

Wind around the walls.

Snow against the shutters.

Fire inside.

Warm blankets.

Quiet breathing.

By morning, very little daylight reached the room.

Clouds and blowing snow covered the sky.

When Eirik opened the outer door slightly, a little snow spilled across the threshold.

He looked outside only briefly before closing it again.

"We are staying home."

No one objected.

There was nowhere they needed to go.

Sigrid prepared porridge.

Astrid checked the wood stacked beside the hearth.

Leif sat near the dogs and continued working on his carving.

The storm moved around the longhouse, but inside, the hours passed gently.

The fire needed tending.

Meals needed preparing.

Wool could be spun.

A torn sleeve needed mending.

A loose handle on a wooden bowl could be repaired.

Eirik inspected several tools.

Astrid braided cord.

Sigrid hummed quietly while she worked.

There was something restful about knowing that the weather had already decided the day for them.

No one needed to travel.

No one needed to go far from the hearth.

The outside world had become snow and wind.

The inside world was firelight, familiar voices, and warm food.

From time to time, Eirik listened to the building.

He knew its ordinary winter sounds.

The soft movement of timbers.

A quiet creak beneath snow.

A faint knock where the wind reached the shutter.

Nothing concerned him.

The longhouse remained warm and solid around them.

By afternoon, Leif grew tired of carving.

He placed the horse beside him and looked toward Astrid.

"Tell me something."

Astrid glanced up.

"What kind of thing?"

"A summer story."

She smiled.

"All right."

Leif pulled his blanket around his shoulders.

Astrid leaned back against the wooden wall and began.

She told him about the fjord beneath the summer sky.

She described the long northern evenings when the light seemed reluctant to leave.

She described green grass around the houses.

Warm stones beside the shore.

Flowers growing along the edges of the fields.

She spoke of bees moving lazily between blossoms.

She remembered the smell of grass after rain.

She described boats gliding across calm water.

Fish hanging on racks beneath the sun.

Children running barefoot near the beach.

Blueberries hidden among low bushes on the hills.

Small streams rushing down from the melting snowfields.

Birds nesting on distant cliffs.

Leif listened with his eyes closed.

Outside, the wind moved steadily around the walls.

Inside, Astrid carried summer through the room with her words.

She described warm earth beneath bare feet.

She described the quiet splash of oars.

She described the evening sun shining across the fjord until the water looked like beaten gold.

By the time she reached the end of the story, Leif was asleep.

His unfinished wooden horse rested beside his hand.

Sigrid looked across the hearth at Astrid and smiled.

The storm continued through another night.

There was plenty of wood.

There was food.

The animals were sheltered.

The family remained close together.

By the next morning, the snow had gathered high against the doorway.

Eirik pushed the door gently.

It opened only a little.

A small wall of snow rested against it.

Astrid joined him.

Together, they pushed farther.

Snow slid inward onto the floor.

They laughed quietly and began clearing it away.

With a wooden shovel and patient work, they opened enough space to step outside.

The wind had already weakened.

Snow still drifted through the air, but the worst of the weather had moved on.

Astrid followed Eirik through the narrow opening.

For a moment, they simply looked around.

The settlement seemed softer than before.

Snowdrifts had rounded every familiar shape.

Fences had almost disappeared.

One storage building looked much smaller because snow had gathered against its walls.

The usual path to the shore was hidden beneath a smooth white slope.

Yet smoke rose from the neighboring houses.

One thin trail.

Then another.

Then another.

Soon doors began to open.

People stepped outside in wool cloaks and boots.

Someone lifted a shovel.

Someone else began clearing snow from a roof.

No one needed to organize the work.

The settlement simply began to uncover itself.

Paths were dug between houses.

Snow was cleared from doorways.

Animal shelters were checked.

Wood was carried where it was needed.

A younger man helped an older neighbor open a blocked entrance.

Two women cleared a path toward the well.

Astrid and Eirik worked beside their own longhouse.

The steady movement warmed them.

They lifted snow, carried it aside, and slowly found the familiar ground beneath.

Leif came outside once the doorway was clear.

He stared at the changed settlement.

"Where did everything go?"

Astrid rested both hands on the handle of her shovel.

"Nowhere."

"But I cannot see the road."

"The road is underneath."

Leif stepped into an untouched drift.

His leg sank deep into the snow.

Astrid took his hand and helped him back onto the cleared path.

"Perhaps stay where the road has returned."

Leif nodded.

For a while.

By afternoon, the clouds began to break apart.

A small patch of pale blue appeared above the mountains.

The wind faded almost entirely.

After several days of constant movement, the silence felt especially gentle.

A raven landed on the exposed top of a fence post.

It shook a little snow from its feathers and called once across the settlement.

People continued working at an easy pace.

There was no need to clear everything in one day.

The important paths were open.

The animals were well.

The houses were warm.

That evening, several families gathered in the largest longhouse.

They brought small things to share.

Dried fish.

Barley bread.

Cheese.

A jar containing the last of some preserved berries.

No one called it a feast.

It was simply an evening together after several days indoors.

A broad fire burned in the hearth.

People found places along the benches.

Children sat beneath blankets.

Someone repaired a wooden spoon.

Someone else braided a length of rope.

An old woman told a story from her childhood.

A man near the far wall hummed an old melody under his breath.

The storm had passed.

Outside, moonlight spread across the fresh snow.

The fjord reflected a pale silver light beneath the winter sky.

Stars returned one by one.

The clouds moved slowly eastward.

Before sleeping, Eirik stepped outside.

Astrid followed him.

They stood beside the longhouse and looked over the quiet settlement.

Smoke rose from several roofs.

Moonlight rested on the snow.

The air was cold and still.

Astrid tucked her hands into her cloak.

"Was that the worst of winter?"

Eirik looked toward her.

A small smile appeared beneath his beard.

"I have learned not to ask winter such questions."

Astrid smiled too.

They went back inside.

The door closed behind them.

The wooden bar settled into place.

The fire had burned low.

Families arranged blankets for the night.

Voices became softer.

Then fewer.

Soon the longhouse held only the gentle sounds of people settling into sleep.

Beyond the walls, the snow lay quiet beneath the moon.

And after the long days of wind, everyone slept deeply.

# Chapter Four — When the Light Returned

Winter did not leave all at once.

It loosened its hold slowly.

So slowly that, at first, almost no one noticed.

Then one morning, Astrid stepped outside and realized that the sky was brighter than it had been only a few days before.

She stood beside the doorway and looked toward the mountains.

The sun appeared behind a ridge.

It did not remain for long.

Still, its light touched the highest peaks and turned the snow a soft shade of gold.

A few days later, sunlight reached the roofs.

Then it reached the upper fields.

Soon it touched the frozen edge of the fjord.

And one morning, a narrow beam of light reached all the way to the doorway of Eirik's longhouse.

Leif stepped directly into it.

He closed his eyes and lifted his face.

"Warm," he said.

Astrid stood behind him.

"It is hardly warm at all."

"It feels warm."

She understood.

After so many weeks of dim winter light, even the weakest sunlight felt generous.

Each day grew a little longer.

Morning arrived earlier.

Evening waited a little later.

The change could be measured in small things.

There was time for one more outdoor task before darkness.

The fire no longer needed to be quite so large during the afternoon.

The goats lingered near the open shelter door.

The dogs slept in patches of sunlight instead of beside the hearth.

Icicles formed along the edge of the roof.

During the warmer part of the day, water began to drip from their tips.

The drops fell steadily into the snow below.

At night, the water froze again.

Winter remained.

But now it carried the first signs of change.

Snow still covered the fields.

The sheltered parts of the fjord still held ice.

The mountains were still white.

Yet the light was different.

Everyone could feel it.

Small birds began appearing around the settlement.

They searched near woodpiles and along the edges of buildings.

Ravens seemed busier than they had in the darker months.

Animal tracks crossed the snow at the forest edge.

One morning, Astrid walked toward a stream that had been hidden beneath snow for most of the winter.

She heard something faint beneath the frozen surface.

She stopped.

Then she knelt.

Under the snow and ice, water was moving.

It was only a small sound.

A gentle trickle beneath the frozen covering.

But Astrid stayed there for a while, listening.

Winter remained above the stream.

Spring had begun underneath.

As the days lengthened, the settlement became more active.

People remained outdoors longer.

Tools repaired during winter were carried into daylight.

Fishing lines were checked again.

Boats were uncovered.

Wooden surfaces were examined after months beneath snow.

Storage buildings were opened more often.

Children dug tunnels into the remaining drifts.

Leif built a large snow wall beside the longhouse.

He spent most of one afternoon shaping it.

The next morning, one side had fallen inward.

He immediately began rebuilding.

Eirik watched him from near the woodpile.

"Important work?"

Leif nodded seriously.

"Very important."

"What does the wall protect?"

Leif looked around for an answer.

At last he said, "Snow."

Eirik nodded.

"Then I hope the snow appreciates it."

Inside the longhouse, the winter food stores were growing smaller.

One sack of barley emptied.

Then another.

Fewer dried fish remained.

The wheels of cheese had become thin pieces.

The turnips were smaller now and needed trimming.

This part of the season required care.

The light made spring seem near.

But there were still cold nights.

Eirik measured the grain.

Sigrid counted what remained.

Astrid checked the storage shelves.

They had enough.

Not an abundance.

But enough.

Meals stayed simple.

Porridge in the morning.

Broth in the evening.

Dried fish.

Barley bread.

A little cheese when there was some to spare.

No one complained.

The food had carried them through the dark months.

Soon other food would return.

Then, one afternoon, a different wind came through the settlement.

Astrid was standing near the shore when she noticed it.

For many months, the wind had felt like winter.

This one did not.

It came from the south.

It was still cool, but softer.

It carried the smell of damp earth, trees, and open water.

Astrid closed her eyes.

The air moved gently across her face.

That day, water fell more quickly from the roofs.

Snow slipped from sun-warmed rocks.

Dark earth appeared around tree trunks.

The paths between houses softened into mud.

Leif stepped directly into the deepest patch he could find.

His boot sank almost to the ankle.

Sigrid watched from the doorway.

"You will clean that before you come inside."

Leif looked down at the mud.

Then at the door.

"Winter was cleaner."

Astrid laughed.

Even the mud felt like good news.

Days passed.

The ice along the fjord began to change.

Dark lines appeared beneath its surface.

Small cracks opened near the shoreline.

From farther out came the occasional deep sound of ice shifting.

Leif ran toward the shore whenever he heard it.

Eirik usually followed.

"No walking on the ice now," he reminded him.

Leif nodded.

He understood.

The ice that had seemed firm for so long was becoming thin and uncertain.

They watched it from the land instead.

Open water appeared near the shore.

Small waves moved against the remaining edge of ice.

The water made a soft, regular sound against stone and frozen snow.

A little more opened each day.

One morning, Astrid woke to a sound she had not heard in months.

Birdsong.

She remained still beneath her blanket.

A small bird sat somewhere near the roof.

Its song was clear in the morning air.

A few notes.

Then silence.

Then another short phrase.

Astrid listened.

The fire near the hearth burned quietly.

Leif was still asleep.

Sigrid rested beneath her blankets.

Eirik had already gone outside.

The bird sang again.

Astrid smiled without opening her eyes.

Later that day, the sun climbed high enough to illuminate the entire settlement.

Snow sparkled on the upper slopes.

Water ran through narrow channels along the paths.

Doors remained open longer.

Blankets were carried outside and laid where sunlight could reach them.

Heavy wool clothing was aired.

Fresh air moved through the longhouses.

Smoke still rose from the roofs, but the fires were smaller now.

Beside Eirik's house, the great woodpile had nearly disappeared.

Astrid stood beside what remained.

She remembered autumn.

She remembered placing the pieces there one by one.

She remembered Leif carrying baskets across the frost.

She remembered the first snowflake landing on Eirik's hand.

Now only a few rows of wood remained.

Eirik came to stand beside her.

He rested one hand on the top piece.

"Enough," he said.

Astrid looked at the small pile.

She knew what he meant.

Enough wood.

Enough food.

Enough wool.

Enough patience.

They had reached the far side of the cold season.

Not because they had defeated winter.

There had never been anything to defeat.

They had simply lived within it.

They had gathered what they needed while the weather was mild.

They had dried food when the days were bright.

They had stored grain.

Collected hay.

Cut wood.

Mended clothing.

Checked the roof.

Fed the animals.

Watched the sky.

Shared what was available.

Kept the hearth warm.

And waited for the light to return.

Much of winter had been made of small things.

A log placed carefully on the fire.

A mitten repaired beside the hearth.

A bowl of warm porridge in the morning.

Hay carried to the goats.

Bread shared with a neighbor.

Snow cleared from a doorway.

A story of summer told while the wind moved outside.

And quiet nights spent beneath wool blankets while the mountains rested under snow.

Now the nights were growing shorter.

The days were stretching open.

Several weeks later, most of the snow had disappeared from the lower ground.

It remained high on the mountains, bright beneath the sun.

Around the settlement, grass emerged.

At first it was flattened and pale.

Then small green shoots appeared.

Tiny flowers opened in sheltered places.

Streams ran freely down the slopes.

Their voices could be heard from far away.

The fjord opened.

Broken pieces of ice drifted toward the sea and slowly vanished.

One evening, Eirik and his family walked down to the shore.

The water was free again.

Dark blue waves moved beneath a pale evening sky.

Several boats rested near the beach.

People had already begun preparing them for the warmer season.

Ropes were checked.

Oars were cleaned.

Wooden hulls were inspected.

Folded sails were taken from storage and opened to the air.

Another season had begun.

Leif picked up a small stone and tossed it into the water.

It landed with a gentle splash.

Ripples widened across the surface.

He watched them disappear.

"Is winter gone?" he asked.

Eirik looked toward the white mountain peaks.

"Mostly."

"Will it come back?"

"Yes."

"When?"

"Not for a long while."

Leif seemed satisfied.

He found another stone but decided not to throw it.

Instead, he placed it carefully on the ground beside him.

The family sat together on a broad rock near the water.

The evening was mild.

The light remained in the sky.

Soon there would be nights when darkness barely came at all.

Soon grass would grow higher around the settlement.

Fish would fill the waters of the fjord.

Birds would gather along the cliffs.

Berries would ripen on the hillsides.

Hay would be cut.

Nets would hang beneath the summer sun.

And much later, when autumn returned, wood would once again be stacked beside the longhouse.

But there was no need to think about that tonight.

Tonight, nothing needed preparing.

Nothing needed counting.

Nothing needed repairing.

The fjord was calm.

The mountains were still.

The evening sky held a soft golden light.

A boat shifted gently beside the shore, its wooden sides moving quietly against the water.

Small waves reached the stones and slipped back again.

Astrid leaned against the sun-warmed rock.

Leif lay beside her with his hands behind his head.

Sigrid watched the changing light on the water.

Eirik rested with his eyes closed.

For a long while, no one spoke.

They listened to the fjord.

They listened to birds moving above the shoreline.

They listened to the distant waterfalls coming down from the mountains, fuller now with melting snow.

They listened to the soft evening wind passing through the grass.

The winter had been long.

Now it was behind them.

The fire in the longhouse could burn low.

The heavy blankets could remain folded during the warmer nights.

The doors could stay open later into the evening.

Water moved freely again.

Above the settlement, a raven crossed the bright sky.

Its dark shape passed slowly over the fjord.

The wings rose and fell.

The bird grew smaller as it traveled toward the mountains.

At last, it disappeared beyond the ridge.

The family remained beside the shore.

There was nowhere they needed to go.

The evening light rested on the water.

Small waves moved in and out among the stones.

Birds called softly from farther along the coast.

From the mountains came the steady murmur of falling water.

The sound was gentle and distant.

Snow was melting high above them.

Drop by drop, it left the mountains.

The drops gathered into tiny streams.

The tiny streams joined larger ones.

Those streams became rivers.

And the rivers traveled down through the valleys toward the fjord.

Winter was becoming water.

Water was becoming river.

River was becoming sea.

The sea received it quietly.

The long cold months softened into running water and evening light.

The last snow in the valley disappeared into the dark earth.

Higher on the slopes, white patches remained, shining softly beneath the sky.

But even they would shrink with each warmer day.

Astrid watched the water without thinking about anything in particular.

Beside her, Leif had become very still.

Perhaps he was watching the clouds.

Perhaps he was nearly asleep.

Sigrid sat with her hands resting loosely in her lap.

Eirik breathed slowly, his face turned toward the last warmth of the sun.

Behind them, the settlement had become quiet.

A little smoke rose from the longhouses.

Someone closed a storage door.

Somewhere, a dog settled into the grass.

The small sounds of evening faded one by one.

Soon the family would walk back to the house.

A few embers would still be glowing in the hearth.

There would be no need for a large fire.

Only a little warmth.

Only enough light to prepare for sleep.

The door would remain open for a while.

Cool air from the fjord would move gently through the room.

Then, when the evening finally grew dimmer, they would close it.

The blankets would be lighter now.

The night would be shorter.

And beyond the walls, water would continue running from the mountains.

The fjord would continue moving against the shore.

Birds would settle into quiet places.

The distant waterfalls would continue beneath the pale northern sky.

There was nothing more to prepare that evening.

Nothing more to carry.

Nothing more to mend.

The wood could remain beside the wall.

The tools could rest.

The fire could burn low.

The family could sleep.

The settlement could sleep.

The long winter had passed into streams and rivers.

The rivers had entered the sea.

And the sea moved slowly beneath the evening light.

The people of the fjord rested at last.

Around them, the northern land grew quiet.

And for now, there was only water.

Only birds.

Only distant waterfalls.

Only the last glow of the fire.

And the soft, peaceful beginning of spring.
