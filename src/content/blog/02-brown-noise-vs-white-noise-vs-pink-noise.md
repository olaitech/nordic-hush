# Brown Noise vs White Noise vs Pink Noise: Which Is Best for Sleep?

White noise is famous.

Pink noise has become increasingly popular.

And brown noise has developed an almost cult-like following among people who prefer something deeper, softer and less hissy at night.

All three are forms of broadband noise, but they do not sound the same. More importantly, there is no single noise color that has been proven to be “best” for everyone.

The useful question is simpler:

**Which sound makes your own bedroom feel least distracting?**

## The quick difference

If you want the short version:

- **White noise:** brighter, hissier, similar to radio static or a strong fan.
- **Pink noise:** softer and more balanced, with less emphasis on high frequencies.
- **Brown noise:** deeper and bass-heavy, often compared with a distant waterfall, aircraft cabin rumble or low rushing sound.

The differences come from how acoustic energy is distributed across frequencies.

Your ears experience that distribution as “color.”

## What is white noise?

White noise contains energy across the audible frequency spectrum, with equal power per unit of frequency.

To most people it sounds bright and consistent:

**sssshhhhhhhh**

That strong upper-frequency content can be useful for masking sharp environmental sounds.

Think of noises such as:

- voices through a wall
- a television in another room
- traffic hiss
- doors closing
- plumbing
- a neighbor moving around upstairs

White noise does not make those sounds disappear. It can make the difference between the background and the interruption less dramatic.

For some sleepers, that is exactly what they want.

For others, the high-frequency hiss feels too intense.

## What is pink noise?

Pink noise also covers a broad range of frequencies, but the energy decreases as frequency rises.

The result usually feels deeper and smoother than white noise.

Nature is often used as an analogy. Rainfall, wind through leaves and ocean-like sounds may contain spectral characteristics that feel closer to pink noise than to white noise, although real nature recordings are not mathematically perfect pink noise.

Pink noise has also received more research attention in sleep science than brown noise.

A 2022 systematic review in the *Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine* looked at studies of white noise, pink noise and mixed auditory stimulation. Positive findings were reported in some studies, particularly in the pink-noise group, but the authors still concluded that the total evidence base was not strong enough for broad clinical claims.

So pink noise is promising, but “promising” is not the same as “proven sleep treatment.”

## What is brown noise?

Brown noise — also called Brownian or red noise — shifts even more energy toward lower frequencies.

It sounds deeper than pink noise.

Many people describe it as:

- a strong distant waterfall
- heavy rain heard through a wall
- low ventilation
- an aircraft cabin
- deep rushing air

If white noise feels sharp to you, brown noise can feel much easier on the ears.

It has become particularly popular online for sleep, relaxation and concentration.

However, there is an important SEO-unfriendly truth worth saying clearly:

**Direct sleep research on brown noise is much thinner than the research on white and pink noise.**

That does not mean brown noise cannot help you relax. It means we should not pretend the scientific evidence is stronger than it is.

## Why any of these sounds might help at night

The most intuitive mechanism is masking.

Environmental sounds can disturb sleep, especially when they are intermittent and unpredictable. Research on road, rail and aircraft noise has repeatedly found associations with sleep disturbance.

A continuous sound can reduce how strongly those interruptions stand out.

The second possible benefit is psychological.

A familiar, unchanging sound gives your attention something neutral to settle into. Unlike speech, social media, television or a podcast, a simple noise bed contains no new information that demands interpretation.

For some people, that makes it easier to disengage from the day.

## Brown noise vs white noise for sleep

Choose **white noise** if:

- your main problem is sharp or inconsistent environmental noise
- you already sleep well with a fan
- higher frequencies do not bother you
- you want strong masking

Try **brown noise** if:

- white noise feels harsh
- you prefer deep, low-frequency sound
- you want something more like a low rumble than a hiss
- your goal is relaxation rather than maximum high-frequency masking

Neither is automatically better.

The best test is to play both at the same low perceived volume for several nights and notice which one disappears from your awareness most easily.

## Pink noise vs white noise for sleep

Pink noise is often a good middle ground.

Compared with white noise, it tends to feel softer and less bright. People who enjoy rain, waves or rustling nature soundscapes may prefer this balance.

White noise may mask some higher-frequency disturbances more aggressively, while pink noise can feel more natural.

Again, preference matters.

The evidence does not justify telling every sleeper to use one particular color.

## Pink noise vs brown noise

This comparison is mostly about depth.

Pink noise still contains a noticeable upper-frequency texture.

Brown noise rolls off much more strongly toward the low end.

If pink noise sounds like a broad, soft wash, brown noise often feels like a distant roar.

A listener who wants something “cozy” and deep may prefer brown. Someone who wants a more natural balance may prefer pink.

## Does the volume matter?

Yes — probably more than the color.

A sleep sound should not become another source of nighttime noise.

Start low.

You should be able to hear it, but it should not demand attention. If the noise feels loud enough to dominate the room, turn it down.

If you are using sound primarily to mask traffic or neighbors, find the lowest level that makes those interruptions less obvious.

That is usually a better target than simply increasing volume.

## Should you use headphones?

You can, but a bedside speaker is often more comfortable for overnight listening.

If you use headphones or earbuds, keep the level conservative. Long exposure to unnecessarily loud audio is not a good trade for relaxation.

A sleep timer is another option if you only need sound during the first part of the night.

## The Nordic Hush way to choose

Do not overthink frequency charts at bedtime.

Try this simple three-night comparison:

**Night 1 — White noise**  
Notice whether it masks the room well or feels too bright.

**Night 2 — Pink noise**  
Notice whether the softer balance feels easier to ignore.

**Night 3 — Brown noise**  
Notice whether the deeper tone feels grounding or too heavy.

Then use the one you thought about the least.

That is often the winner.

## What about rain instead?

If synthetic noise feels sterile, try rain.

Rain combines continuous masking with natural variation. It can sit somewhere between the predictability of generated noise and the emotional comfort of a familiar natural environment.

For some sleepers, this makes rain more inviting than any noise color.

## The answer: there is no universal best noise color

White, pink and brown noise are tools.

White noise is bright and effective at creating a consistent acoustic background.

Pink noise is softer and often closer to the spectral feel of nature.

Brown noise is deeper and popular among listeners who dislike high-frequency hiss.

Science has not established a universal winner, and individual responses vary.

The best sleep sound is not the one with the most impressive explanation.

It is the one that quietly does its job and then gets out of the way.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is brown noise better than white noise for sleep?
There is not enough evidence to say that brown noise is generally better. Brown noise sounds deeper and may be more comfortable for some listeners, while white noise can provide stronger high-frequency masking.

### Which noise color has the strongest sleep research?
White and pink noise have been studied more than brown noise. Even for white and pink noise, systematic reviews describe the evidence as mixed and limited.

### Can I play brown noise all night?
Many people do, but keep the level low. You can also use a timer if you only want it while falling asleep.

### Is rain basically pink noise?
Not exactly. Real rain is acoustically complex, but steady rainfall can share the softer, lower-frequency emphasis that makes pink noise pleasant to many listeners.
